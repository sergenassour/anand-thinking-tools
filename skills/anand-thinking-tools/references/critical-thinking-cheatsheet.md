# Critical thinking cheat sheet (Paul and Elder, as adapted by Anand Vengurlekar)

Transcribed from the printed handout "Critical Thinking: Key Leadership Reflection" passed around the
workshop. It summarises Richard Paul and Linda Elder's 1990s work on the elements and standards of
thought, arranged as a checklist. The presenter is influenced by the checklist manifesto (aviation crew
training, WHO surgical checklists): busy, tired people do not run a 24-cell review by hand, so the AI
runs the sweep and hands back questions to reflect on.

Footer on the handout: "Beware Level 4 thinking".

How to use this file: Tool 3 in SKILL.md. Pick one red-flag family (default: hazy), walk the eight
elements, and for each flag you find write a reflection question anchored to the document.

## Contents

1. What we think: the eight elements of thought
2. How well we think: the standards
3. Red flags: hazy, bias and logic factors by element
4. Worked prompt (as demonstrated in the workshop)
5. Red-team variant for meetings

## 1. What we think: the eight elements of thought

Every act of thinking has all eight. Apply the standards in section 2 to each of these.

| Element | Reflection question |
|---|---|
| Purpose | What am I trying to accomplish? |
| Issue at hand | What problem am I addressing? |
| Information | What data, facts, experiences am I focusing on? |
| Interpretation | What inferences or conclusions am I drawing? |
| Concepts | What frameworks or "laws" are shaping my thinking? |
| Assumptions | What am I taking for granted? |
| Implications | What are the consequences of my thinking? |
| Point of view | What is my dogma? |

The presenter's gloss: information includes heuristics from your past as well as data and evidence, and
biases sit in there too. Concepts range from mathematics to the culture you come from. Assumptions include
the ones you dismiss as well as the ones you take for granted.

## 2. How well we think: the standards

| Standard | Reflection question |
|---|---|
| Clarity | Could you elaborate further? |
| Accuracy | How can we check that? |
| Precision | What data, facts, experiences am I focusing on? (The handout repeats the Information line here; Paul and Elder's usual precision question is "Could you be more specific?") |
| Relevance | How does this help us with the issue at hand? |
| Depth | What factors make this a difficult problem? |
| Breadth | Do we need to look at this from another perspective? |
| Logic | Does all this make sense together? |

Handout note: "In meetings, assign one person to red team by representing and asking the above (don't
hate them!)."

## 3. Red flags by element

Three families: **hazy** factors (vague, unclear), **bias** factors (slanted, self-serving), **logic**
factors (does not hold together). Rows follow the order of the elements table above (the printed handout
lists point of view before implications in this table; the content is identical).

| Element | Hazy factors | Bias factors | Logic factors |
|---|---|---|---|
| Purpose | **Vague goals**: using fluffy words like "synergy" or "optimisation" without working meaning or metrics. | **Hidden agendas**: the stated goal doesn't align with the actual resource allocation. | **Scope creep**: trying to solve five unrelated problems with one initiative. |
| Issue at hand | **Loaded questions**: the question is framed to force a "yes" (e.g. "How do we implement X?"). | **The wrong level**: asking a tactical question when the problem is strategic, or vice versa. | **Obsolete issues**: solving a problem that was solved by a competitor two years ago. |
| Information | **Cherry-picking**: only using data that supports the desired outcome (see groupthink checklist). | **Dated data**: relying on "last year" market numbers in a volatile field. | **Anecdotal evidence**: generalising a global strategy from one customer complaint. |
| Interpretation | **A "big leap"**: the conclusion requires a massive jump in logic not found in the data. | **Correlation vs causation**: assuming that because B followed A, A caused B. | **Over-generalisation**: assuming what worked in one market will work in all markets. |
| Concepts | **The wrong map**: applying a "manufacturing" framework to a "creative" problem, or vice versa. | **Jargon masks**: using technical terms to hide a personal lack of actual understanding. | **Rigid thinking**: refusing to look outside a single discipline (e.g. only looking at the finance issue). |
| Assumptions | **Unstated beliefs**: e.g. "the market will always grow" as an absolute fact. | **Stereotyping**: basing strategy on outdated assumptions about user behaviour. | **The "status quo" bias**: assuming that because things have been this way, they must stay this way. |
| Implications | **Optimism bias**: only listing the "best-case" outcomes. | **The domino effect**: ignoring how a change in Department A changes Department B. | **Short-termism**: solving today's crisis by creating a massive challenge for next year. |
| Point of view | **The echo chamber**: only considering the perspective of the leadership team (see groupthink). | **Blind spots**: ignoring the "villain" or "competitor" perspective. | **Narrow lens**: looking at a global problem through a purely local or regional filter. |

## 4. Worked prompt (as demonstrated)

Sources loaded: a 30-page consulting proposal (Gatwick Airport) plus this cheat sheet.

> Based on the hazy column factors from the critical thinking cheat sheet, examine the [Gatwick]
> proposal and provide me with hazy-factor questions to reflect on.

What came back: questions, each with a reference into the proposal. The presenter's point: ask for
**questions to reflect on**, not for a list of flaws. The person reading the questions knows the context
the proposal is going into (the client, the history, the politics); the AI does not. So the right
questions, grounded in Paul and Elder, let the human do better thinking than either could alone.

Second and third passes: repeat for bias factors and logic factors.

Turning a flag into a reflection question, examples:

| Flag found | Weak output (verdict) | Strong output (reflection question) |
|---|---|---|
| Vague goal in purpose | "The objective is vague." | "Section 1.2 promises 'operational excellence'. What would the client measure at month twelve to know it arrived? (ref p.3)" |
| Loaded question in issue | "The question is loaded." | "The brief asks 'how do we implement the new roster'. Who decided the roster is the problem, and what would change if it were the shift pattern? (ref p.5)" |
| Optimism bias in implications | "No downside cases." | "Appendix B models the upside only. What is the first thing that goes wrong in month one, and who notices? (ref p.27)" |

## 5. Red-team variant for meetings

Assign one person to represent the standards. Their job is to ask the questions in section 2 and look for
the flags in section 3 out loud, and everyone agrees in advance not to hate them for it. When a user asks
for this, draft that person's question list for the specific agenda, ordered by the element most likely to
be weak for that kind of decision (purpose and assumptions for strategy, information and interpretation for
analysis, implications and point of view for change programmes).

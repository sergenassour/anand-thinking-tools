# Incubation: the four stages

Graham Wallas, co-founder of the LSE, published *The Art of Thought* in 1926 after studying mathematicians
who worked a problem, failed, left it in disgust, and then had the answer arrive unbidden. The pattern he
named is incubation. The workshop's claim: once you know the stages, you can **systemise** incubation
instead of waiting for it, and you can stop being embarrassed that your best ideas come in the shower.

Why it works: in the shower, on a walk, driving, in sport, or falling asleep, the conscious mind is running
an automatic routine. The problem is not being actively worked, so the subconscious (creativity,
imagination, wisdom) gets to work on it. Meetings rarely produce the best idea; the walk afterwards does.
People who step out "for fresh air" come back asking for a post-it.

The US Marines, an action-first organisation, still teach reflection as a core tool. Reflecting at your
desk can look like a "career limiting move", which is why the desk-based variants below matter.

## The four stages

The slide text, expanded with the presenter's commentary.

### 1. The heavy load (preparation)

Research, gather data, map out constraints, and actively try to solve the problem. Read every document,
every reference, the statement of the problem. Do nothing but overload the conscious mind with the issue.

**Hit the wall**: work until you hit a literal or mental dead end. This friction is the exact trigger your
subconscious needs to register the problem as "important".

How to help: build the brief. One-sentence problem statement plus three alternative framings; every hard
constraint; every relevant document pulled into one place; what a solution must satisfy; what has already
been tried and why it failed. Density over tidiness.

### 2. Intentional detachment (incubation)

This is where the mental work shifts from conscious effort to subconscious processing (your wisdom). You
must step **completely** away from the primary problem.

Do not switch to another high-stress task. Instead, engage in undemanding "habit" activity that allows
your mind to wander: a walk, the dishes, a shower. Critically, **do not look at your phone, do not read,
do not do anything else that engages cognition**. Those steal the processing.

While the conscious mind rests, the subconscious enters a state of "combinatory play", connecting disparate
memories and new data points to form unique solutions.

How to help: ask what already works for the user and do not judge it. Help them schedule it deliberately
(10 to 15 minutes, phone out of reach). Everyone's incubation is different.

### 3. The catch (illumination)

The breakthrough, the classic "aha" moment, occurs when the subconscious finishes restructuring the
information and passes the solution back to the conscious mind.

Breakthroughs almost always happen when you are thinking about something else entirely. Because these
insights are fleeting, **capture them immediately in writing** before they slip away.

How to help: when a user arrives with "I just had a thought", write it down verbatim first. Then help them
unpack it. Do not evaluate it yet; that is stage four.

### 4. Grounding (verification)

A subconscious breakthrough is just a hypothesis until you prove it. Bring the idea back into the
analytical world. Test it against your original constraints, gather feedback, and prototype it to ensure
it is both practical and effective. If flaws are found, use that feedback to restart the cycle back at
step one.

How to help: run the critical-thinking review (Tool 3) on the new idea, then name the cheapest experiment
that would falsify it.

## Detachment routines that came up

- Shower, walk, driving, sport, falling asleep (the room's answers).
- Fresh air during a break.
- The presenter's desk routine: a pot of tea and two chocolate digestive biscuits (kept in the fridge),
  three or four plants, and a small fish tank. Ten to fifteen minutes watching the fish grow and sipping
  tea, phone untouched. "I'm not suggesting you get a fish tank. Don't be embarrassed by whatever works
  for you, and systemise it."
- Andrew Huberman's four-step desk protocol for incubation was referenced as further reading. The slide
  was not photographed, so do not quote steps from it; point the user to Huberman's material on
  incubation and creativity if they want the neuroscience version.

## The overnight variant

For a presentation or decision the next day: review the material with great intensity the night before,
sleep, and in the morning spend five to ten minutes making the changes that now feel obvious, before
looking at anything else. Not for everything, but reliable for prepared performances.

## Diagnosing where a user is

| What they say | Likely stage | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| "I keep going round in circles" | Never finished the heavy load, or refusing to detach | Build the brief, then schedule detachment |
| "I've read everything and I'm fried" | Wall reached | Stop. Detach now. No phone. |
| Both of the above at once | Wall reached, brief never written down | Close the brief in under ten minutes from what they already have (no new research), then detach |
| "I had an idea in the car but it's gone" | Missed catch | Reconstruct what they were thinking about just before; next time, capture immediately |
| "I've got this idea, is it any good?" | Catch made, needs grounding | Verbatim capture, then Tool 3, then cheapest falsifying test |

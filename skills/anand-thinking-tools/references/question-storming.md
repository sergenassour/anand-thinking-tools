# Question-storming: innovation through questions nobody asked

## The claim

People think innovation means coming up with cool new ideas. The history of innovation suggests otherwise:
it is coming up with **questions** that nobody else thought to ask. Once the right question exists, the
answer becomes an engineering problem. Steve Jobs did not know how to navigate music with a thumb; he knew
that if the question could be answered, the industry would change.

Three iPod generations in a row kept changing the interface. The question driving it: when you hold
something in your hand, the only thing free is your thumb, so **how do you navigate your music with just
your thumb?** The iPod destroyed the Sony Walkman business in about fourteen months; Sony closed the
factories because even Sony staff were buying iPods.

## The examples

| Company | The question | What it became |
|---|---|---|
| Uber (literally in the business plan) | Why do I have to stand in the rain looking for a taxi when my phone knows exactly where I am? | Location-based ride hailing |
| Amazon | Why should filling in billing and delivery details take longer than it needs to? | One-click checkout |
| Spotify (its CEO, speaking in Scandinavia) | Why do I have to buy a song when I only want to listen to it once? | Streaming |
| Apple, iPod | How do you navigate your music with just your thumb? | The click wheel |
| Apple, iPhone | Why do we need a revolutionary user interface? What's wrong with the existing ones? Who wants a stylus? | Multi-touch |

Young Steve Jobs, in an early interview, on the question that matters most: in most companies when someone
new asks "why is it done this way", the answer is "because that's the way we do it here" or "that's the way
it has always been done". His view was that the biggest contribution of quality thinking is to approach
those processes and ask why. Not "yes sir, no sir", but "wait, let's step back".

## Anatomy of the first iPhone launch

The keynote is structured almost entirely as questions, each answered by the product:

- Why do we need a revolutionary user interface? (The smartest phones have fixed plastic keyboards
  whether you need them or not.)
- What happens when you think of a great idea six months from now? You can't add a button.
- How do you solve this? Get rid of the buttons and make a giant screen.
- How do we communicate with it? We don't want a mouse. A stylus? Who wants a stylus? Nobody.
- So what do we use? The pointing device we are all born with: fingers.

Anyone at Nokia or BlackBerry watching that would have felt their product become junk overnight. When a
user is writing a pitch or launch narrative, offer to structure it the same way: the chain of questions
the audience is already half-asking, each answered in turn.

## The Socratic tradition

Asking questions deliberately instead of assuming the answer is fixed. Constantly, constantly asking new
questions. This is the stance the whole workshop asks for: your edge is no longer new ideas or regurgitated
knowledge, because AI gives those to anyone. Your edge is the question.

## Lenses for generating questions

Work through these against the status quo the user described. Aim for questions, never solutions.

1. **The customer's friction**: where does the user of this thing stand in the rain? What do they tolerate
   because everyone does?
2. **The fixed constraint**: what is assumed unchangeable (the keyboard, the billing form, the album)? Why?
3. **The thing in the hand**: what physical or situational fact about the user has nobody designed for?
4. **The step everyone accepts**: which step would a newcomer find absurd? Why does it still exist?
5. **The newcomer's question**: what would someone on their first day ask that the veterans have stopped
   asking?
6. **The incumbent's unaskable question**: what question would hurt the current business model most if it
   were answered honestly?
7. **The one-time use**: where do we make people buy or build the whole thing when they want a slice?
8. **The information already present**: what does the system already know that the user still has to
   supply by hand?

## Ranking

Score each question on two axes: how uncomfortable it is for the incumbent (including the user's own
organisation) and whether it is now answerable with current technology or resources. The best questions
score high on both. Return the top three to seven with a one-line rationale and the lens tag. Then stop.
The user's wisdom decides which to pursue.

# Reading with AI: Adler plus Feynman

Reading has become hard. People read a page or two, reach for the phone, and discover they do not know what
they read. Instead of saying "put the phone down", the workshop's answer is to read **with** the AI, using
two older methods: Mortimer Adler's *How to Read a Book* (how to x-ray a book before and while reading it)
and Richard Feynman's test of understanding (explain it to a twelve-year-old without technical terms).

How to use this file: Tool 5 in SKILL.md. Pick the mode, run the prompts, and in chapter-reading mode
stay quiet until asked.

## 1. Adler's levels of reading

Adler wrote in the 1950s, before digital books, and his advice was physical: look at the table of
contents, go to the index to see which terms recur, read the first paragraph of each chapter. The levels:

1. **Basic comprehension**: start at the beginning, read to the end.
2. **Inspectional reading**: x-ray the book before committing to it. Two sub-modes:
   - **Systematic skimming**: title, preface, contents, index, publisher's blurb, pivotal chapters, a few passages.
   - **Superficial reading**: read straight through once without stopping for what you do not understand.
3. **Analytical reading**: the full, slow, questioning read.

(Adler also describes syntopical reading, comparing several books on one subject. The workshop covered
this indirectly through loading several books into one notebook and asking all the authors a question.)

The AI shortcut: "Give me a systematic skim of this book according to Adler." Because the AI knows Adler
and, in many cases, the book, any book in front of you can be inspected in minutes.

## 2. Inspectional reading with AI

| Adler's step | Questions to ask the AI |
|---|---|
| 1. Examine the title and preface | What is the central subject of this book? What problem is the author attempting to solve? What does the author promise the reader? |
| 2. Study the table of contents | How is the argument structured? What are the principal themes? How does each section contribute to the overall purpose? |
| 3. Examine the index | What are the 10 to 15 most important concepts, thinkers or recurring terms? Which subjects receive the greatest attention? |
| 4. Read the publisher's description | What is the book's central proposition? Who is its intended audience? What distinguishes it from other books on the subject? |
| 5. Identify pivotal chapters | Which chapters appear essential to understanding the author's argument? Why are these particularly important? |
| 6. Sample important passages | Identify passages that appear to contain the author's central claims, definitions or conclusions. Explain their significance briefly. |

Demonstrated prompt:

> I would like to begin reading *Seeing What Others Don't* by Gary Klein. According to Adler's definition
> of inspectional reading, can you tell me which are the pivotal chapters I should read to understand the
> author's argument?

When answering as the AI: say which chapters, in what order, and give the central proposition in no more
than two sentences. If you only know the book by reputation rather than text, say so and mark the
uncertainty rather than inventing chapter titles.

## 3. Reading a chapter

Use before and after each chapter. Part 1 is also the "what's coming up" preview that makes a chapter
easier to read, the way a film you have seen twenty times is easy to watch because you know what comes
next.

**Part 1: inspectional reading (before)**
- **Purpose**: what fundamental question is the author attempting to answer?
- **Structure**: how is the chapter organised? Identify the principal sections and their functions.
- **Central proposition**: what is the author's main argument, expressed in no more than two sentences?
- **Key concepts**: identify the five most important concepts and briefly define them as the author uses them.
- **Argument sequence**: outline the three to five principal steps in the author's reasoning.
- **Evidence**: identify the most important examples, illustrations or evidence used.
- **Conclusion**: what does the author ultimately want the reader to understand?

**Part 2: preparation for analytical reading (after, or while reading)**
- **Difficulties**: which arguments or concepts warrant closer examination?
- **Assumptions**: what assumptions appear necessary for the author's argument to hold?
- **Critical questions**: formulate three questions I should consider when evaluating the chapter.

**Reading alongside the user**: the user says "I'm reading this chapter. I'll be silent, but I'll ask you
questions as I go." Then they read. Answer in context ("what does that term mean in paragraph three"),
briefly, and go quiet again. The patience is the point; do not fill silence with summaries they did not
ask for. Voice mode suits this well.

## 4. Combining Adler and Feynman: the three prompts

After reading, the user puts the book down and gives one of these. The AI responds with what was missed.

1. "I will explain to you my summary of what the author is trying to explain. Give me your feedback."
2. "I will give you the author's central argument in my own words. Tell me what I have missed."
3. "I will tell you what I think are the main ways that the author supports his argument. Tell me what I
   have missed."

How to respond:
- Listen to the whole summary before responding.
- Say what they got right in one line, then what is missing, then what is distorted. Be specific: name the
  concept, the chapter, the example.
- Plain language, no grades, no "great job". The workshop praised the AI's non-judgemental patience; keep
  it. Do not pad with reassurance either; the user wants the gap, not therapy.
- If they used the author's jargon, ask them to say it again without the technical term. That is the
  Feynman test.

## 5. Adler's four questions (self-test)

Adler's test of whether you understood a book (or a chapter):

1. What is the book about as a whole?
2. What is being said in detail, and how?
3. Is the book true, in whole or in part?
4. What of it? (What follows for you?)

Use these as a quiz: ask one at a time, let the user answer, and respond as in section 4.

## 6. The Feynman method

Richard Feynman explained physics from gravitation to quantum mechanics without technical terms. His
study method's core test: put the book down and explain the content as if to a twelve-year-old, using
none of the technical vocabulary. If you cannot, you have not understood it yet; go back to the part you
stumbled on. The scientific attitude underneath it: guess, compute the consequences, compare with
observation; if it disagrees with experiment, it is wrong.

## 7. Working with sources

The workshop demonstrated loading a stack of books and documents (as PDFs) into one notebook tool and
asking all of them a question at once:

> To all the authors: what do they say about limited resources and their effect on strategy?

The two strategy books named: Lawrence Freedman, *Strategy: A History*, and Richard Rumelt, *Good Strategy
Bad Strategy*. Other uses: all of a stakeholder's press releases and public speeches for a PR strategy;
a client's document stack under NDA before a flight; whitepapers alongside the book they cite.

Calibre converts ePub files the user already owns into PDF. The presenter was explicit that converting
DRM-protected Kindle books is illegal; do not help with that. Work from material the user legitimately has.

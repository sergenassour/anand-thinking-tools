## The framing stories (useful when explaining why a tool matters)

### Sesame Street and the distractor method: build on the addiction, don't fight it

In the 1960s and 70s the concern was TV addiction among poor children who had no books. The superficial
response was "switch off the TV and read". Instead, researchers funded by the Carnegie Corporation
(Children's Television Workshop; Robert Freed Bales, pioneer of one-way-mirror observation; Edward Palmer)
watched children in front of TVs. After ten minutes TV became background noise, with one exception:
**adverts**. Children of every background were engrossed by 60 and 90 second commercials.

Their insight: if children will attend to 60 to 90 second ads, make educational material the same shape.
Palmer's **distractor method**: a child, a researcher, a slide carousel changing every 7.5 seconds to
compete for attention, and a monitor showing test segments. Anything that held less than 80% of the
child's attention against the slides was dropped. The show reached about 85% attention. Then they hired
Jim Henson, who had spent fifteen years making puppets for advertising.

The lesson for this skill: nothing here says AI or phones are bad. The point is to build on what people
already do (phones, screens, AI) and make the thinking better inside those habits. Participants'
self-reported screen time ranged from 2.3 to 8 hours a day.

### The phone on the table

Research cited: a phone that is switched off and face down still reduces cognitive performance by roughly
10 to 15%. Moving it to another room recovers only about 5%. Nobody in the room would put theirs in
another room. (Used to explain why detachment in incubation must mean no phone at all.)

### Spell check, the floppy disk, Wikipedia

Microsoft Word 6 (1993) introduced automatic spell check; nobody refuses it to prove their spelling.
Teenagers do not know the save icon is a 3.5-inch diskette. Students were told never to use Wikipedia.
Every generation's tool was going to ruin thinking. A client once rang to report a spelling mistake in the
presenter's proposal and said, relieved, "now I know you wrote it, not AI".

### The London black cab and "the Knowledge": wisdom over regurgitation

To drive a London black cab you pass the Knowledge: 320 routes, 640 quarter-mile areas, 25,000 streets
within a six-mile radius of Charing Cross, every point of interest (and by the end, what is showing at
each theatre). Two to four years of daily study, oral exams, studying on a scooter. MRI studies show the
hippocampus physically changes.

The room was asked: now that GPS exists, is this a total waste of time? Some said yes (not convenient,
GPS has live traffic). Others: it builds the brain; they are also tourist guides; and drivers say the
memorised experience gives an intangible understanding that GPS cannot replace.

Then the turn: everyone in the room has a career of thousands of emails, meetings and projects that rivals
the Knowledge. "Do we just get rid of you and replace you with the equivalent of GPS?" Everything in the
workshop depends on recognising that you have **wisdom**, even though "I'm really wise" sounds dodgy at a
dinner party.

### Learning velocity and Bloom's taxonomy

Learning velocity: the ability to identify a gap, find the sources, understand the knowledge and begin to
apply it, faster than other people and other companies. The pressure predates AI.

Bloom's taxonomy (1950s, revised 2001) runs remembering, understanding, applying, analysing, evaluating,
creating. The workshop targets understanding, analysing and evaluating.

## The six tools in the order taught

1. Dual-column note taking (left: what is said; right: what your wisdom says).
2. Asking questions (iPod, Jobs interview, Uber, Amazon, Spotify, iPhone keynote, Socratic tradition).
3. Critical thinking with Paul and Elder and a notebook AI (Gemini Notebook / NotebookLM demo: proposal
   plus cheat sheet, prompt for hazy-factor questions to reflect on; cross-book questions to Freedman and
   Rumelt; Calibre for ePub to PDF; multi-format sources including YouTube and websites; free tier 30
   sources, paid 300; the user chooses which sources to query, which is the edge over a plain chat).
4. Reflection and incubation (Wallas 1926, four stages, Huberman's desk protocol, tea and fish tank).
5. Reading with AI (Adler's levels, inspectional reading prompts, reading a chapter, voice mode
   patience, Feynman, the three combined prompts, Adler's four questions).
6. Narrative structures and storyboard-first (eleven structures; "choose the structure, feed the document
   plus the structure to Claude, ask for storyboard only with sections, titles and slide titles; adjust;
   then ask for the deck". Claude was named as the recommended tool for this step.)
